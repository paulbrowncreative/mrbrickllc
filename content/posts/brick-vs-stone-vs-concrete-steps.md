Front steps take more abuse than almost anything else on a house: foot traffic, snow shovels, salt, and frost pushing up from below every winter. When it's time to replace them, the three common choices are **poured concrete**, **brick with stone or concrete treads**, and **natural stone**. Here's how they compare for a Southeastern Michigan home.

[[photo:stone-steps-iron-railings|Brick risers with stone treads leading to a porch with iron railings.]]

## Poured concrete steps

**Pros:** Durable, economical, and quick to build. A broom finish gives good traction in snow and rain, and concrete can be formed to almost any layout.

**Cons:** A plain look on a brick house, unless it's dressed up with brick or stone cheeks or a decorative finish. New concrete is vulnerable to salt in its first winter. See [new concrete care](/blog/new-concrete-curing-and-first-winter).

**Best for:** Side and rear entries, budget-conscious projects, and modern or simple home styles.

[[photo:concrete-block-steps|New poured concrete steps at a side entry.]]

## Brick steps with stone or concrete treads

**Pros:** The classic choice for a brick home. Brick risers tie the steps to the house, and stone or concrete treads give a durable, walkable surface.

**Cons:** More joints to maintain over time. Brick as the walking surface wears and spalls faster than stone treads in our climate.

**Best for:** Brick homes, historic neighborhoods, and anyone matching an existing brick porch.

[[photo:brick-steps-stone-treads|New brick steps with tan stone treads.]]

## Natural stone steps

**Pros:** The most premium look and excellent wear resistance. Heavy stone treads feel solid underfoot and age well.

**Cons:** Material and labor typically cost the most, and sourcing and cutting take more time.

**Best for:** Grand entries, stone-front homes, and projects where the entry is a focal point.

## What matters more than the material

Whatever you choose, these details decide whether steps stay safe and level:

- **A footing or base that won't heave.** Steps attached to a porch should move with the porch, and standalone steps need their own footing below frost depth. Read [why frost depth matters](/blog/frost-depth-footings-michigan).
- **Consistent step heights.** A single riser that's even slightly taller than the rest is what trips people. Rise and run should be consistent from bottom to top, and your city may have code requirements.
- **Treads that shed water.** A slight pitch keeps water, and ice, from sitting on the steps.
- **Solid railing anchors** set in sound masonry, not added later into thin concrete.
