A retaining wall does one of the hardest jobs in a landscape: holding back tons of soil, and whatever water is in that soil, every day of the year. When one starts to lean, bulge or crack, the cause is almost always the same handful of problems. Fortunately, the same handful of details builds a wall that lasts.

## Reason 1: Water pressure

Wet soil is heavy, and water trapped behind a wall pushes much harder than dry soil. In Michigan, that water also freezes, adding more pressure every winter. **Missing or clogged drainage is the most common reason retaining walls fail.**

A lasting wall has:

- **Drainage aggregate** (clean stone) directly behind the wall
- A **perforated drain pipe** at the base to carry water away
- **Fabric** where needed to keep soil from clogging the stone
- **Surface grading** that sends runoff around the wall, not over or behind it

## Reason 2: A weak base

A wall that sits on soft soil, or on a thin, uncompacted base, will settle unevenly and tip. The base needs to be excavated, built from compacted aggregate or a footing as the wall requires, and set level.

## Reason 3: Not enough reinforcement for the height

Short garden walls can often stand on their own weight. Taller walls need more: geogrid reinforcement layered into the soil behind segmental walls, or steel reinforcement and grouted cores in block walls. The taller the wall, the more critical this is. Tall walls, and walls holding up driveways, slopes or structures, may need engineering.

## Reason 4: Frost

A wall built without regard for frost can heave and shift each winter. Proper base depth and drainage keep freeze-thaw movement to a minimum. Read [why frost depth matters](/blog/frost-depth-footings-michigan).

## Signs your wall is failing

- **Leaning or bulging** outward
- **Cracks or separated blocks**, or gaps opening between courses
- **Soil washing out** at the base or through joints
- **Standing water** behind or at the base of the wall
- **Tilted or displaced caps**

## Repair or rebuild?

Minor issues, like a loose cap or a small section that's shifted, can sometimes be repaired. A wall that's leaning or bulging along its length usually needs to be taken down and rebuilt with the drainage, base and reinforcement it was missing.
